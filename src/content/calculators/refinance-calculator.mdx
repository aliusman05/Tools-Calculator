Use this refinance calculator to find out whether a new loan is worth what it costs to get. Enter the loan you have now, the offer you are considering and the closing costs, and you will see the break-even point: the month when refinancing starts to put money back in your pocket. It works for mortgages and for other fixed-rate loans such as car or personal loans.

## How to use this calculator

1. Enter the **balance you still owe** and your **current rate**, and how much time is **left** on the loan. The current payment is worked out from these, so check that it matches your statement.
2. Enter the **new rate** and the **new term**. Use the term the lender is offering, not the time you have left.
3. Add the **closing costs**, including any discount points, and choose whether you pay them in cash or add them to the loan.
4. Set how many **years you expect to keep** the new loan. This drives the "if you keep it" figure. If you might sell or refinance again soon, use a smaller number.

## How it works

Both loans are run month by month as ordinary fixed-rate loans. For each month the calculator adds up what you have paid and what you still owe, for the old loan and for the new one, and subtracts the closing costs you paid in cash:

```
net benefit = (paid + owed on current loan) − (paid + owed on new loan + cash costs)
```

The **break-even point** is the first month the net benefit is zero or more. Counting the balance you still owe is what keeps the comparison honest. If you refinance into a longer loan, your payment drops and your cash flow improves, but you also owe more later, and the net benefit shows both.

When the closing costs are paid in cash and your payment falls, the calculator also shows the **quick estimate** (closing costs ÷ monthly saving) that lenders usually quote. The two can differ because a lower rate cuts interest by more than it cuts the payment, since a smaller payment also pays down less principal.

## Worked example

You owe **$300,000** at **7%** with **25 years** left, and you are offered **5.5%** for **25 years** with **$4,500** in closing costs paid in cash.

- Your payment drops from **$2,120.34** to **$1,842.26**, a saving of **$278.08** a month.
- The quick estimate, closing costs divided by that saving, is **16.2 months**, about 16.
- Counting the balance you still owe, the break-even is **month 13**, because in the first month interest falls by $375 ($1,750 to $1,375) while the payment falls by $278.
- If you keep the loan for 7 years you come out about **$26,607** ahead. Over the full 25 years the gap is about **$78,923**, with interest of **$252,679** on the new loan against **$336,101** left on the old one.

Now change the offer. You owe **$250,000** at **6.5%** with **20 years** left, and a lender offers **5.75%** over a fresh **30 years**, with **$6,000** of costs added to the loan.

- The payment falls from **$1,863.93** to **$1,493.95**, which is **$369.99** a month.
- But the loan is bigger and 10 years longer. Interest rises from **$197,344** to **$281,821**.
- There is **no break-even**, and over the full terms you finish about **$90,477** behind.

A lower payment and a better deal are not the same thing, which is why the calculator reports both.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Compare the same horizon.** The longer you keep the new loan, the more a lower rate pays off. If you expect to move in two or three years, test that number.
- **Watch the term.** Restarting a 30-year clock after ten years of payments can erase the saving. Try a new term close to the time you have left.
- **Include every fee.** A "no cost" refinance often has a higher rate instead. Enter that rate and zero costs to compare it with an offer that has costs.
- **Check your current balance.** Use the payoff figure from your lender, not the original loan amount.
- **Get the loan estimate in writing.** Rates and fees change by lender and by day. Treat this as a first pass, not a quote.
