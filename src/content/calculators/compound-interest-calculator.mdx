Compound interest is what makes steady saving powerful: your interest earns interest of its own. This calculator shows how a starting balance and regular monthly deposits can grow over time, how much of the final balance is your own money, and how much is interest. Try different rates, time periods and compounding schedules to see what matters most.

## How to use this calculator

1. Enter your **starting amount** and the **monthly deposit** you plan to add. Either can be zero, but not both.
2. Set the **annual interest rate** and the **time** in years.
3. Choose how often interest **compounds**: daily, monthly, quarterly or annually.
4. Choose whether deposits are made at the **start** or **end** of each month.
5. Read the future value, then explore the chart and the year-by-year table.

## How it works

For one lump sum, the textbook formula is:

```
A = P × (1 + r / m)^(m × t)
```

- **P** is the starting amount.
- **r** is the annual rate as a decimal.
- **m** is how many times a year interest compounds (365, 12, 4 or 1).
- **t** is the time in years.

With monthly deposits, the calculator steps through each month using the equivalent monthly growth rate, adds the deposit at the start or end of the month, and keeps a running total. That produces the same answer as the lump-sum formula plus the future value of the deposits, and it lets the table show every year.

## Worked example

You start with **$10,000**, add **$200 at the end of each month**, and earn **5%** compounded monthly for **10 years**.

- Your deposits total **$24,000** over the decade.
- The starting amount alone would grow to **$16,470.09**.
- The deposits alone would grow to **$31,056.46**.
- The final balance is **$47,526.55**, of which **$13,526.55** is interest.

Compounding schedule matters less than you might expect: $10,000 at 5% for 10 years becomes **$16,288.95** with annual compounding and **$16,486.65** with daily compounding, a gap of under $200.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Start early.** Time is the biggest driver. Ten more years can matter more than a higher rate.
- **Be realistic about the rate.** A savings account and a stock fund earn very different long-run rates and carry different risk.
- **Remember what is not included.** Taxes, fees and inflation will reduce what your money can buy.
- **Match the compounding to your account.** Check whether your bank compounds daily or monthly and use the same setting here.
- **Increase deposits when you can.** Raising your deposit by even a small amount every year adds up.
