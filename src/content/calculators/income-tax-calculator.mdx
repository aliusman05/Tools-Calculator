## How to use this calculator

1. Pick your **filing status**: single, married filing jointly, head of household, or married filing separately.
2. Enter your **total income** and any **pre-tax contributions** such as a traditional 401(k), IRA or HSA.
3. Keep the **standard deduction**, or choose **itemized** and enter the total. If you are 65 or older or blind, add that under the standard deduction.
4. Enter any **tax credits** and the **tax already withheld** if you want to see an estimated refund or balance due.
5. Read your estimated tax and the bracket table below the result.

## How it works

Taxable income is calculated first:

```
Taxable income = Income − Pre-tax adjustments − Deduction
```

The tax is then the sum of each bracket's slice of taxable income multiplied by that bracket's rate. For a single filer in 2026 the first $12,400 is taxed at 10%, the amount from $12,400 to $50,400 at 12%, from $50,400 to $105,700 at 22%, and so on through 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. Credits are then subtracted from the tax, but not below zero.

The effective rate is total tax divided by total income, and the marginal rate is the rate of the highest bracket your taxable income reaches. All the figures, the brackets, the standard deductions and the extra amounts for age or blindness, live in one data file with a source reference and a date.

## Worked example

A single filer earns **$85,000**, takes the **2026 standard deduction of $16,100**, and has no other adjustments.

- Taxable income: $85,000 − $16,100 = **$68,900**.
- 10% on the first $12,400 = **$1,240**.
- 12% on the next $38,000 (to $50,400) = **$4,560**.
- 22% on the remaining $18,500 = **$4,070**.
- Total federal income tax: **$9,870**, an effective rate of about **11.6%** and a marginal rate of **22%**.

For comparison, a married couple filing jointly with **$150,000** of income has taxable income of $117,800 after the $32,200 standard deduction and owes about **$15,340**.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Do not confuse marginal and effective rates.** A raise into a higher bracket does not tax your whole income at the higher rate.
- **Use pre-tax contributions.** Money into a traditional 401(k) or HSA lowers taxable income, which is why they are popular.
- **Compare deductions.** Check whether itemizing beats the standard deduction for your situation.
- **Remember withholding.** A big refund usually just means you overpaid during the year.
- **Check your state.** State and local income taxes are separate and not included here.
